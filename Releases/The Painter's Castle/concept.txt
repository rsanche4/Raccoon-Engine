Working Title: [The Painter's Castle / Untitled Knight Game]
Genre: Cozy dungeon-crawler / exploration, first-person Engine target: Raccoon Engine (DOOM-style raycaster) — intended as a test project for engine features Visual style: Simple flat visuals Tone reference: Yume Nikki (surreal, dreamlike exploration) + Undertale (cozy, emotionally warm) + Dante's Inferno (structural/thematic backbone)

Logline
A knight crosses the ocean of death, pays the ferryman, and enters a vast, surreal castle-world to rescue his princess from a grim reaper–like captor — collecting paintings, unlocking doors, and fighting through increasingly strange and hostile floors before a final confrontation with a Satan-like final boss at the world's altar.

Core Fantasy
You are a lone knight with a sword. The world is enormous, strange, and not fully explicable — you're not meant to understand all of it, just to keep moving toward her.

The Hook / Premise
You awaken somewhere in the world, prompted into your quest by a short phrase.
Your princess has been taken by a grim-reaper-like figure.
The world is themed like an inverted hell: hellish premise, innocent/dreamlike presentation.
You cross an ocean to reach it and pay a ferryman a coin — this may double as your way out at the end.
World Structure
Main hub area: roughly 10 floors × 10 rooms, interconnected in non-obvious (possibly non-Euclidean) ways. Exact room count is flexible — may end up smaller than the full 100.
Hidden worlds: additional worlds branch off from rooms within the main hub, initially inaccessible until unlocked — these hidden worlds are where a lot of paintings likely live, not just the main 100 rooms.
Doors gate progress: clearing certain doors/rooms unlocks access to others.
Each room may contain: a puzzle, a beast encounter, or a collectible (painting).
Content approach: No procedural generation — every room/world is hand-designed, with AI used as a speed tool during design (not as a runtime generator). This keeps content quality and tone consistent but means the scope should be planned around what's realistically hand-buildable.

Core Loop
Enter a room/world through a door.
Solve a puzzle, fight a beast, or explore.
Find/collect a painting (or other reward).
Return to full health / save at designated points.
Use collected progress to unlock further doors.
Repeat until the "ultimate door" requirement is met.
Mechanics
Combat: Full melee kit — slash, defend/block, throw sword (must be retrieved), pick sword back up. Goal is for it to feel satisfying and varied, not just a single attack verb — this needs dedicated feel/juice work (hit-stop, sound, animation) so it doesn't go stale over many encounters.
Collectibles: Paintings, scattered across the main hub and hidden worlds. 666 was a thematic placeholder, not a locked target — actual count will depend on how much content actually gets built (see content approach above).
Progression gate: The final/ultimate door requires collecting some threshold of paintings to open.
Life/health & death: Mario-style — losing all health sends you back to your last checkpoint rather than wiping progress. No permadeath, no full resets.
Saving: Game is saveable.
Painting secrets: Some paintings may contain a darker hidden narrative layer.
NPC messages: The princess leaves messages throughout the world — recurring emotional throughline ("you will see your girl").
Ending
After collecting the required paintings and opening the ultimate door, the player frees the princess.
Escape is through the world's altar, back out of the underworld.
A large final antagonist (Satan-like) attempts to stop the escape — climax/final encounter.
Tone
Consistently strange throughout, rather than "innocent → dark" linear progression — the world can be happy/whimsical and unsettling/dark in the same breath, room to room. The contrast itself is the tone, not a ramp.

Open Design Questions
Painting distribution: Roughly how are paintings spread across the main hub vs. hidden worlds? Worth mapping even loosely once room count is finalized.
Combat feel: Slash/defend/throw/retrieve is a solid kit on paper — the open question is execution (hit feedback, enemy variety, encounter pacing) so it stays fun across many rooms.
Scope vs. ambition: Since everything is hand-built, worth periodically checking planned room/world count against actual build velocity.
Non-Euclidean doors: Worth a small isolated prototype in Raccoon Engine before committing the whole map to self-warping geometry, given the engine's raycasting constraints — also a good general level-design testbed for the engine.
Checkpoint placement: Where checkpoints sit (per room, per floor, per world) determines how punishing death actually feels in practice.
Suggested Next Steps
 Prototype one "test room" in Raccoon Engine to validate door-warping/non-Euclidean feel and general level design
 Build out the core combat kit (slash/defend/throw/retrieve) as an early testable loop
 Decide checkpoint placement/frequency
 Draft a rough floor-by-floor outline for the main hub, noting where hidden-world branches connect
 Pin down what the "darker secret" inside paintings actually is
